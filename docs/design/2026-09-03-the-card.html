What I want you to judge

Eight calls on the card. Two are yours. Turn Notes on, or click an item, to find each on the page.

  1. The card expands in place and the column widens to 560px. The card's top edge does not move; the columns to its right slide over. No modal, no side panel, no scroll. At 288px a plan's sentences break every six words and the card becomes unreadable — which is why 0.1's expand was only ever skimmed. A modal would have solved the width and lost the board, and losing the board is losing the point. Yours: 560px is my number. If you want the open card wider — say 720, with two columns of neighbours pushed off-screen — that is your call about how much board you want to keep while reading one card.
  2. The first thing on an open card is what it makes true, in your words. Not the plan's intent — the card's own SERVES sentence, at 14.5px, ahead of everything. Your intent says a card opens to its intent and essence without reading code. The plan's own Intent section for this card reaches a line of code within a paragraph: true, necessary, and not what you are here for. So the owner-facing sentence leads and the plan's text sits one section down, whole and unedited. Mine. Where a card has no SERVES row of its own — a card just born from a new plan — the first sentence of the plan's Intent takes its place, marked as such.
  3. The brief and the record are two sections, not one list. The brief is what is true before the work: TODAY, COST, YOUR TIME, WAITS. The record is what is true after: DELIVERED, WATCH, REVIEW, RULED. 0.1 rendered all of them as one run of labelled rows, so a shipped card and a pending card looked identical until you read the labels. Splitting them means the shape of a card tells you where it is in its life from three feet away. Mine. The two lists are fixed and named in the types, so a new row kind must declare which half it belongs to.
  4. An empty section says so rather than disappearing. A card with nothing written back still shows The record, saying nothing is there yet. Sections that vanish move every other section, so the same fact lives in a different place on every card and you re-hunt each time. A fixed skeleton costs one line of grey and buys a board you read by position. Yours: this is the one place I spend vertical space on nothing. If you would rather the empty sections collapse, say so.
  5. The plan is rendered from the file, not stored. Title, Status, Effort gate with its reasoning, Sequencing, and the Intent section — read at the moment you looked, with the age of the read shown. The document is the substance; the board holds only what a document cannot. Rendering means a plan edited in the repo is right on the board a second later with nobody syncing anything. The gate's reasoning is there because it is what you are confirming when you press Start in slice 03 — the level alone tells you nothing. Mine.
  6. History starts at the import, and says so. Every change to a card is a row: what moved, from where, to where, when, and by whom — you, a session, or the machine. 0.1 kept none of this, so the record begins on day one and is honest about it. Mine, and it is in the store from the first commit rather than added later. A move that leaves no trace cannot be measured later, and the moves most worth reading are the ones nobody remembers making.
  7. The actions row holds only what this slice can honestly do. Open the plan · Copy path · Move to. Nothing else. Start, Discuss, Answer and Watch belong in this row and arrive with the runtime in slices 02 and 03. A disabled button promising them would be a lie with a tooltip, and 0.1 taught that a door that does not prove its effect is worse than no door. Mine.
  8. WATCH renders as your move, in amber, on a shipped card. It is the only amber on a card that has otherwise landed. "Done is a closed loop, not a claim." The WATCH row is the named signal that has to arrive before a card may leave — so it has to look like something owed, not like a footnote under DELIVERED. In slice 03 the board or a session reads that signal and moves the card itself; until then it is amber and it is yours. Mine.

The five sections, in this order, on every card, always: what it makes true · the brief · the record · the plan · history. A card that has nothing for a section says so. This fixed skeleton is the whole reason a session with no memory can be handed a card and know where to look — and the reason you can rank a column without reading it twice.
